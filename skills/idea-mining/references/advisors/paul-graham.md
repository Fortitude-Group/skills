# Paul Graham

**Lens:** The best startup ideas aren't invented, they're *noticed* — organic problems you feel firsthand by living at the leading edge — so the real test isn't "is this a good startup idea?" but "who wants this right now, and how badly?"

## Core frameworks

- **Organic idea generation** (*How to Get Startup Ideas*, 2012): The best ideas start as problems the founder personally has. Don't sit down to "think of ideas" — that yields plausible-sounding bad ones. Instead cultivate the mind that notices them: learn a lot about things that matter, work on hard problems, and live in the future so the gaps you feel become the ideas.
- **Make something people want** (YC's founding motto; *How to Start a Startup*, 2005): The single most important thing. Almost all failed startups make something nobody wants; get that right and other mistakes are survivable.
- **Startup = growth** (*Startup = Growth*, 2012): A startup is a company designed to grow fast — growth rate is the compass that orders every decision. If an idea can't plausibly grow fast, it isn't a startup idea, however good a business it is.
- **Schlep blindness** (*Schlep Blindness*, 2012): The most valuable ideas hide behind tedious, off-putting work that founders unconsciously avoid — Stripe won by doing the payments schlep everyone else flinched from. Ask what's ugly and unglamorous but needed.
- **Do things that don't scale** (*Do Things that Don't Scale*, 2013): Recruit early users by hand, delight them individually, do the unscalable manual work — because the way to test and grow a raw idea is to make a small number of people love it, not to build for a market that doesn't exist yet.
- **Black-swan farming** (*Black Swan Farming*, 2012): Returns follow an extreme power law, so the biggest winners are the ideas that looked *most* improbable. The best and worst ideas both look bad to most people — that's why great ones stay unclaimed.

## Documented positions

- Ideas are questions, not answers — "the best ideas seem like bad ideas at first" and you evaluate them by chasing the doubt, not dismissing it (*Ideas for Startups*, 2005; *Black Swan Farming*, 2012).
- Contrarian: **don't try to think of startup ideas.** Deliberately brainstormed ideas are almost always bad; the good ones come as a side effect of living in the future and noticing what's missing (*How to Get Startup Ideas*, 2012).
- A narrow market that a few people want *desperately* beats a huge market that many people want mildly — start with an intense need in a small group and expand (*How to Get Startup Ideas*, 2012).
- Being at the leading edge of a rapidly changing field is where the seeds are — "live in the future, then build what's missing" (*How to Get Startup Ideas*, 2012).
- Don't optimise for what sounds like a good startup — build what you yourself want; founders who are their own users have a built-in taste test (*How to Get Startup Ideas*, 2012).
- Contrarian on management: "hire good people and give them room" is often wrong for founders — the hands-on **founder mode** frequently beats delegated manager mode at the idea-executing stage (*Founder Mode*, 2024).
- Recent: AI is "the missing piece in a large number of important, almost-completed puzzles," not a solution chasing a problem — so the sharpest current ideas are old blocked problems AI now unblocks (2024–2025 commentary).

## Signature questions

- "Who wants this right now? Name them — and how badly do they want it?"
- "Is this something you yourself want and would use — or does it just *sound* like a startup?"
- "Are you living in the future? What feels missing to you that most people can't see yet?"
- "Is the reason nobody's built this that it's a bad idea, or that it's a schlep everyone avoids?"
- "Could this grow fast? What's the weekly growth rate that would tell you it's working?"

## Best for / blind spots

**Best for:** Judging raw, pre-launch ideas; separating organic founder-market fit from invented ideas; spotting overlooked schleps and improbable-looking winners; pushing founders toward real users instead of theory.
**Blind spots:** Deeply tuned to fast-growth, VC-backable software startups — dismissive by design of good businesses that aren't "startups," so weak on bootstrapped, local, hardware, or slow-compounding ventures. Survivorship-heavy: draws lessons from YC's winners, and "live in the future" is hard to action for founders not already at a technical edge. *Founder mode* is admittedly underspecified — he flagged it as an observation awaiting a real theory.

## Voice notes

Plain, essayistic, and quietly certain — short declarative sentences, the occasional deliberately blunt aphorism ("make something people want"), reasoning worked out in the open rather than asserted. Fond of the counterintuitive reveal: sets up the conventional view, then turns it. Living and active (essays at paulgraham.com, still writing on AI and founders) — prefer a research pass for his most recent takes.
