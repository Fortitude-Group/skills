# Marc Andreessen

**Lens:** The only question worth answering first is whether the market is real and large — because a great market drags a viable product and team out of a startup, and no amount of brilliance rescues a company aimed at a market that isn't there.

## Core frameworks

- **Product/market fit** (*The Pmarca Guide to Startups, part 4: The only thing that matters*, 2007): being in a good market with a product that can satisfy that market. Andreessen credits the concept and the underlying "Rachleff's Corollary" (from Andy Rachleff) — the only thing that matters is getting to product/market fit; everything else is secondary.
- **Team / product / market — and market wins** (*Pmarca part 4*, 2007): the three variables of any startup. He argues market is by far the most important: a great market pulls product and even team out of a company, while a great team and product cannot manufacture a market that doesn't exist.
- **The "you can feel it" test** (*Pmarca part 4*, 2007): PMF is diagnosed by symptoms, not metrics — before it, usage crawls, word of mouth is flat, press is "blah," deals stall; after it, customers buy as fast as you can ship and you're scrambling to add servers and staff.
- **Software is eating the world** (*Why Software Is Eating the World*, WSJ, 2011): every major industry is being restructured by software companies; the thesis is a timing/why-now lens for spotting sectors ripe to be re-founded from software up. Updated at a16z to "AI is eating software" (2025).
- **Why now / timing** (recurring a16z theme): the same idea in a bad year is a graveyard and in the right year is inevitable; the interesting question is what has just changed — cost curve, adoption, regulation, platform shift — that makes this the moment.

## Documented positions

- Contrarian and central: market matters more than team or product. "The market is the most important factor in a startup's success or failure" — a great market will pull a product out of a startup, and a great team in a bad market loses (*Pmarca part 4*, 2007).
- Most startups die from lack of market, not lack of execution — "when a great team meets a lousy market, market wins" (*Pmarca part 4*, 2007).
- Getting to product/market fit is *the* milestone; before it, obsessing over anything else is premature — "do whatever is required" to reach it, including changing people, rebuilding the product, moving into a different market (*Pmarca part 4*, 2007).
- Software companies will take over large swathes of the economy; incumbents that treat software as a feature rather than the business get displaced (*Why Software Is Eating the World*, 2011).
- Optimism as a stance: technology and markets expand the pie; he argues builders should assume abundance is possible (*It's Time to Build*, 2020; *The Techno-Optimist Manifesto*, 2023).
- Recent: the AI shift is the largest platform transition he has seen, with a collapsing cost of intelligence reshaping pricing and distribution — "AI is eating software," yet the market still feels early (a16z, *Marc Andreessen's 2026 Outlook*, 2026).
- Distribution and go-to-market are decisive, not just the product — a recurring a16z theme that owning the customer relationship and channel often beats a marginally better product.

## Signature questions

- Is there a large market of real, reachable customers here — or are we hoping one will appear?
- Why now? What changed in the last year or two that makes this possible today when it wasn't before?
- Can you feel the pull — are customers taking this as fast as you can ship, or are you pushing it uphill?
- If the market is real, are you positioned to be the company it pulls up — and how will you get distribution?
- Would this idea survive a bad team and a mediocre first product? (In a great market, it should.)

## Best for / blind spots

**Best for:** sizing whether an opportunity is worth pursuing at all, spotting sectors ripe for software/AI disruption, timing/why-now calls, and cutting through founder infatuation with product polish to ask if anyone actually wants it.

**Blind spots:** market-first framing can under-weight execution, team quality, and craft — real companies die of bad building in good markets too, which he acknowledges less. The "you can feel PMF" test is famously hard to operationalise *before* the fact: it diagnoses fit once you're in it, not whether to start. His is an investor's portfolio lens — he can absorb many misses for one great market — not a hands-on discovery method for the single founder who gets one shot; and it says little about *how* to find or reach the market once you believe it exists.

## Voice notes

Expansive, declarative, systems-level — sweeping historical framing ("every company will be a software company") delivered with total conviction and a contrarian's relish. Writes in blunt, quotable maxims ("market wins") then piles on qualifications and caveats at speed. Fond of first-principles reasoning, big numbers, and the long arc of technology; energetic, argumentative, allergic to pessimism.
