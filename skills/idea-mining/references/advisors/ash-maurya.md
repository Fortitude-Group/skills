# Ash Maurya

**Lens:** A business plan is just a stack of untested assumptions, so the job of an early idea is not to build it but to find the one or two assumptions that will kill it and test those first — cheaply, on real customers, before writing code.

## Core frameworks

- **Lean Canvas** (*Running Lean*, 2010; leanstack.com): A one-page, nine-box business model captured in ~20 minutes — Problem, Customer Segments, Unique Value Proposition, Solution, Channels, Revenue Streams, Cost Structure, Key Metrics, Unfair Advantage. Maurya's adaptation of Alexander Osterwalder's Business Model Canvas, swapping four boxes (Key Partners/Activities/Resources/Relationships) for the ones that actually kill startups: Problem, Solution, Key Metrics, Unfair Advantage.
- **Riskiest-assumption-first** (*Running Lean*, 2010): List everything the model assumes to be true, rank by risk (what would sink the venture if wrong × how uncertain you are), and design the cheapest experiment against the top one. Builds directly on Steve Blank's customer development and Eric Ries's Lean Startup build-measure-learn loop.
- **Problem/solution fit → product/market fit → scale** (*Running Lean*, 2010): Three sequential stages. First prove people have a problem worth solving (before building), then prove your product solves it (product/market fit — the one milestone that matters), only then pour on growth. Don't optimise a stage you haven't reached.
- **Customer Factory** (*Scaling Lean*, 2016): Model the business as a system that turns unaware visitors into happy customers across five macro-steps — Acquisition, Activation, Retention, Revenue, Referral (a pirate-metrics AARRR lineage) — so you can see where the factory leaks rather than guessing.
- **Traction Roadmap / systematic value creation** (*Scaling Lean*, 2016): Work back from a business-model-goal (e.g. the throughput needed for a viable business), set the trajectory, and manage against that leading number rather than vanity metrics. Traction is the single metric that matters to the model's survival.

## Documented positions

- "Love the problem, not the solution." — his signature line; founders over-invest in a solution they're attached to instead of validating the problem is real and worth money (*Running Lean*; leanstack.com talks).
- "Life's too short to build something nobody wants." — the whole method exists to avoid the most common startup death (*Running Lean*).
- Contrarian: most startups fail not because they can't build the product but because they build the *wrong* product — execution isn't the risk, unvalidated assumptions are (*Running Lean* introduction).
- A business plan is a document written by an entrepreneur before they know if the business works — i.e. "an artifact of a set of untested assumptions." Fill a Lean Canvas in 20 minutes instead and go test it (*Running Lean*).
- Talk to customers before you build. Problem-interviews and solution-interviews come first; the "get out of the building" discipline is inherited from Steve Blank and made concrete with scripts (*Running Lean*).
- Product/market fit is the only milestone that matters early — everything before it is search, everything after is growth; don't scale a leaky factory (*Scaling Lean*).
- Pick the *right* riskiest assumption, not the most convenient one to test — teams default to testing what's easy (feasibility) rather than what's fatal (demand/viability) (leanstack blog).
- Speed, learning, and focus beat raw funding; more money spent on an unvalidated model just buys a bigger failure faster (*Scaling Lean*).

## Signature questions

- "What has to be true for this to work — and which of those, if false, kills it?"
- "Have you actually talked to the customers who have this problem, or are you in love with your solution?"
- "What's the cheapest experiment that would falsify your riskiest assumption this week?"
- "Which stage are you really at — problem/solution fit or product/market fit — and are you optimising the right one?"
- "Where does the customer factory leak: acquisition, activation, retention, revenue, or referral?"

## Best for / blind spots

**Best for:** early-stage software and app ideas, converting a vague pitch into falsifiable assumptions, sequencing validation experiments, customer-interview discipline, spotting solution-love before it burns runway.
**Blind spots:** the canvas-and-assumption discipline can decay into box-filling and experiment theatre — a tidy Lean Canvas feels like progress without a single customer conversation. Strongest for fast-iteration software where a test is cheap; lighter on deep-tech/hardware/biotech where the riskiest assumption may take years and millions to test, and on brand, taste, and category-creation plays that don't reduce cleanly to a falsifiable experiment.
